The traffic-cam plate tracker can't be built. Here's the version that can.

You asked for an app that scans public traffic cameras to find any license plate, traces its path with time codes, and shows its speed per section — for parents, fleets, dealers, and police. I researched federal and 50-state law, app-store policy, and what traffic cameras can actually do. The literal idea is a dead end on four independent grounds. The goal behind it is a real, fundable business — once you change the data source and the consent model.

Verdict

The original mechanism fails on capability (traffic cams can't read plates and there's no national feed), federal law (the DPPA bars plate→owner lookups for these buyers), state law (ALPR bans + electronic-tracking statutes; the "register the plate" gate supplies none of the required consent), and platform policy (Apple/Google ban covert tracking). PlatePath keeps the goal and rebuilds it as consent-based, owner-verified vehicle route intelligence — legal in all 50 states, App-Store-approvable, and serving the same four customers.

What dies, and what ships

✕ What can't be built

  • Scanning public traffic cameras for a plate — they're low-res, wide-angle, snapshot-only, mostly unrecorded, with no national API.
  • A plate → registered-owner lookup for parents/dealers/fleets — barred by the DPPA.
  • Tracking "any plate you type in" — the register-a-plate gate stops nothing; it's textbook stalking.
  • A consumer download that traces a car — rejected by Apple & Google.

✓ What ships instead

  • Route + time codes + per-segment speed from consented telematics (GPS/OBD/phone) — full coverage, accurate speed.
  • Tracking only on vehicles whose ownership you've verified — a real gate, not a text box.
  • Driver-disclosure ledger, per-read audit log, per-state retention controls.
  • Fits the permitted lanes: dealer/BHPH, fleet, family, and an off-store agency tier.

Can it be built? (feasibility)

Real plate-based tracking exists — but not on the substrate the idea assumes. DOT/511 traffic-flow cameras are engineered not to read plates: low resolution by design, wide pan-tilt-zoom fields of view, snapshots every few seconds to minutes rather than video, usually not archived, and split across ~50 incompatible state systems with no national API. You cannot reconstruct a path or compute segment speed from them.

A precise caveat, because it matters for credibility: some government cameras do read plates — electronic tolling, red-light/speed cameras, and point-to-point "average-speed" enforcement that literally computes segment speed from two timestamped reads. So it isn't physics that stops you — it's access. Those systems, and the real plate-reader networks (Flock: 120,000+ cameras across 49 states, ~$300M ARR, ~$7.5–8.4B valuation; Motorola/Vigilant; Rekor), are closed and gated to law enforcement and DPPA-permissible commercial users. None is a feed a consumer app can tap.

The working substrate is consented GPS/telematics — the proven approach for "trace the path and speed," with full coverage and no plate-reading problem. That is what PlatePath is built on, and the engine that computes it is tested and running in this repo.

Where plate detection does belong — the checkpoint view. At a camera you operate (a dealer lot gate, a fleet yard, a lawful agency ALPR site), PlatePath renders the classic detection frame — box every vehicle, call out the plate — but with one rule that makes it legal: it reads the plate only for vehicles registered to your account. Your car gets the red box, the plate, and the speed; every other vehicle is boxed but stays anonymous. Reading everyone's plate off a public road is the illegal part; identifying your own verified vehicle at your own checkpoint is fine — and looks identical for the car you're allowed to see. The register-a-plate gate is the core mistake. Registration isn't ownership, and ownership isn't the driver's consent — anyone can type an ex-partner's plate. The federal DPPA (18 U.S.C. §§2721–2725) even makes the plate→owner lookup you'd use to "verify" it unlawful, with a private right of action at $2,500/violation. State electronic-tracking statutes (CA §637.7, TX §16.06, FL §934.425) require the tracked person's consent — which PlatePath's design supplies and the original doesn't. Four states (NH, ME, AR, VT) ban private ALPR outright; retention caps as short as ~3 minutes (NH) make historical tracing impossible there. Full analysis and citations: docs/LEGAL.md.

App Store reality: Apple and Google prohibit covert tracking. The only approvable lanes are parental (verified minors), enterprise/fleet (company vehicles with driver notice), and law-enforcement (sold off-store under legal process). PlatePath is designed to fit exactly these.

Is the need real? (market)

The original mechanism has no legitimate market — but the underlying jobs are real and funded. Fleets are pushed onto telematics by the FMCSA ELD mandate; 72% of US parents already use monitoring tools (Pew, 2025); BHPH/subprime dealers carry ~$32B in loans with subprime at a multi-year-high 15.31% of financing (Experian, Q4 2025) and already run GPS on collateral.
